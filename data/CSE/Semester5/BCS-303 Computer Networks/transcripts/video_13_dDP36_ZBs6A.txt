Next is the collision domain. If we find the collision domain, we are finding the collision domain in the bridge. Can a collision occur in the bridge? No. A collision can occur in hubs and repeaters, but it won't occur in bridges. The reason is that bridges use the store and forward strategy. 

The store and forward strategy means that it stores the packet and forwards it after processing. So store and forward means that the bridge has its own buffer or memory. So if a packet arrives, then it stores it in the buffer. At the same time, another packet arrives, then it stores it also in the buffer. There will be no collision. 

So if we talk comparatively, in a repeater or hub, a collision can be maximum "N" ("N" means maximum collision can occur), but in bridges, collisions will be very rare as compared to that because the bridge has its own buffer which uses the store and forward technique. 

And the last one here is the bridge protocol data unit. Bridges use the bridge protocol data unit, and the reason is that it is used to remove loops. Loops mean that many times packets get stuck in loops. When there are multiple paths, then it'll get stuck in the loops. To remove this loop, we have a TTL field in the router, i.e., the Time to Live field. But in the bridge, there is no such field. So bridges create a spanning tree, and they create a spanning tree based on this protocol, and the spanning tree makes them find out on which side to forward the packet. There occurs no loop in a spanning tree. So with the help of those spanning trees, they remove the loops so that the packet won't stay stuck in the loops permanently.
