Deadlock, what is a deadlock? If two or more processes are waiting on the happening of some event, but that event doesn't happen, that is called a deadlock, and those two processes are in the deadlock state. To understand by a simple example, let's say that I want to open an account in a bank. I go to the bank and tell the bank personnel that I want to open an account, but that person is saying that first you have to deposit this minimum amount, then we will open the account. But I am saying that first I will open the account, after that, if I want, I can submit the money. But what is that? A deadlock situation. Because what I am saying is that definitely I will give the money, but open the account first. But what the bank personnel are saying is first you deposit money to us, then we will open the account. So both are waiting on the happening of some event, but that event will not occur. That is a deadlock situation.

Let's look at one more example. There are two cars, A and B, and two drivers are driving those cars. A is saying that I am on the right direction, and he is moving towards this direction. At the same time, some person B is also moving towards A. Let's say that B is moving on the wrong side—this happens in India normally—where I as A think that I am going in my direction, but B is also thinking that my direction is also this. So in this situation, what will happen? Both are waiting for some event. What is the event? A says that I want to go in this direction, and B is saying I want to go in this direction. But how will they go? Either A will have to take the car back, or B will have to take the car back. But why will they do it? Ego is a thing. So that's why neither A will remove the car nor B will remove it. That is a kind of deadlock situation. Both are stuck. Both are stuck here. This situation is known as a deadlock situation.

Or if we talk in technical terms, then in synchronization also we had talked about deadlock. Like there are two semaphores, S1 and S2. There is a process P1, and it wants two semaphores, S1 and S2. And there is one more process P2, which wants S2 and S1. So let's say that P1 has taken the semaphore S1 and is waiting for S2, and P2 is having semaphore S2 and is waiting for S1. That is again called a deadlock situation because P1 got one semaphore S1 but didn't get S2. Why didn't it get S2? Maybe because of sleep, maybe it got preempted here, so P2 got S2, but when it was getting S1, it also went to sleep, meaning control went back to P1. So P1 has S1 and says I want S2, and P2 has S2 and says I want S1. So both are waiting on the happening of some event. What is that event? That P1 gets S2 and P2 gets S1. But they won't get them because P1 made S1 down, P2 made S2 down, and both are in the blocked state. Now who will unblock them? No one. That is called a deadlock situation.

If we see with a simple resource example, let's say there are resources R1 and R2, and two processes P1 and P2. Let's say that R1 is allocated to P1. This line is showing that P1 has taken R1, and P1 is saying that I need R2 because P1 is requesting R2. And R2 is held by P2, and P2 is saying I need R1. So this situation is what? This situation is a kind of deadlock. It is not exactly a deadlock. Now we can't call it a deadlock. Why we can't call it a deadlock will be clear to you now. 

Here we are saying that P1 is sitting with a resource R1, P1 is using R1, but saying that I will execute if I get R2. P1 is saying I'm having R1, but please give me R2. If you give R2 to me, then I will get executed, but R2 is already taken by P2. And P2 is also saying I will get executed, but just give me R1 because it is requesting R1. But it won't get it. Why? Because P1 will not execute. When will P1 execute? When it gets R2. And it has R1, but when it gets R2 it will execute. And when will P2 execute? When it gets R1, but it won't get R1 because P1 has R1, and P1 will not get R2 because P2 is holding R2. So this is a kind of deadlock situation.

However, we can't call it a strict deadlock yet because for a deadlock there are four necessary conditions. The conditions are mutual exclusion, no pre-emption, hold and wait, and circular wait. These four are necessary conditions for a deadlock. 

What is the first condition? Mutual exclusion. Mutual exclusion means whatever resource you are using in the scenario, that resource must be used in a mutually exclusive way. It should not happen that more than one process can also use that resource at a time. Take a printer, for example. If there is a process that is using the printer—meaning one process is using the printer to print something—and at the same time one more process comes, then P1, the first process, will not be preempted, meaning it will not be stopped there. It is not like P2 is also working in between, meaning P2's pages are printing and P1's pages are printing, both printing at the same time. No, this should not be there. These are necessary conditions; if out of these four any condition does not hold true, then there is a chance that a deadlock may not occur. So mutual exclusion means that all resources must be used by all processes in a mutually exclusive manner, one by one. Interleaving is not allowed.

Second, no pre-emption. No pre-emption means that if P1 is using R1—R1 is allocated to P1—and P1 is saying I need R2, and P2 is saying I need R1, pre-emption is not there. Pre-emption means it shouldn't happen that one process is holding some resource and another process comes and we preempt the first process, telling it to release its resources. No. Pre-emption will not be there. Here there is no priority system where if high priority comes, low priority is stopped; we are talking about equal priority here. So that's why pre-emption will also not occur. One process which is holding will keep holding till it executes; it will not be preempted, meaning it will not be switched.

The third one is hold and wait. This is a situation of hold and wait. Hold and wait means P1 is holding one resource, that is R1, and waiting for R2, and P2 is holding one resource, that is R2, and waiting for R1. This is hold and wait. It should not happen that P1 says that I want R2 and it releases R1, meaning it releases what it was holding. No, whatever it is already holding must remain with P1, and it can request more resources. One resource or two resources, as many as P1 has, it will hold them and wait for the other resources which it doesn't have.

The fourth one is circular wait. Circular wait means a kind of circle. A circle means there is a loop in this. If there is a loop here, then definitely a deadlock situation can occur here. I am still saying it *can* occur because all four conditions are very important; after these four conditions, I can decide whether a deadlock is there or not.

Let's see one more example of circular wait. Let's say that there is a process P1 which is holding some resource R1. This arrow means—if this arrow is going towards the process, we call it "hold," meaning that process is holding that resource, meaning it is allocated. So R1 is allocated to the P1 process. There is a process P2 which is saying "I need R1." "Need" means I am requesting R1; for a request, we draw an arrow towards the resource. P2 is requesting R1 and holding some resource R2. Let's say there is another process P3 which is requesting R2 and it is holding R3. Meaning R3 is held, and P3 is requesting R2. And P1, which is holding R1, is requesting a resource R3. 

So here we have a circular wait: P3 is having R3 and waiting for R2, P2 is having R2 and waiting for R1, and P1 is having R1 and waiting for R3. This is a kind of circular wait, a loop. These four conditions are necessary conditions, which we call necessary and sufficient conditions to become a deadlock situation. Not one situation alone—mutual exclusion, no pre-emption, hold and wait, and circular wait—if all four necessary conditions are present, then we can say a deadlock is occurring. So these four conditions are very important. This is a simple explanation of what a deadlock is and what the conditions of a deadlock are. Thank you.
