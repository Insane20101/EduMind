Scheduling algorithms are a way of selecting a process from the ready queue and putting it in the CPU. According to the degree of multiprogramming, we try to keep multiple processes in the ready queue, and the ready queue lies in the RAM. We use different queues in operating systems to execute processes and bring as many processes as possible into the ready queue. Because the ready queue lies in RAM, I can also say that when we have a lot of processes in RAM, now what do we do with those processes? Obviously, we have to execute those processes. And to execute those processes, I have to select multiple processes among them and execute them on the CPU. 

If we assume that we are using a uniprocessor system—a uniprocessor system means that we have just one CPU—I can say one CPU can execute one process at a time. So what criteria can we use to get any one process executed? That is the scheduling algorithm. They help decide which process should be executed and for how much time. I've already written all these categorizations; these are the different scheduling algorithms. Broadly, they are of two types: pre-emptive and non pre-emptive. 

Pre-emptive and non pre-emptive—these terms get used so much in scheduling algorithms, and this comes up in a lot of numericals also. Many times, students get confused by these terms. I'm explaining this through a very simple point of view. A pre-emptive process is that if I took out a process from RAM and placed it in the running queue (i.e., the CPU)—meaning I gave it to the CPU, and the CPU will execute that process—now, it's possible that the process which is getting executed is still in the running state. I can pick up that process during the running state and place it again in the ready queue. This means a process that was running properly, I'll stop it in between and will give some other process to the CPU, placing the first process again in the ready queue so that it will get executed again in the future. Now, why we use it and what the concept is, we'll discuss further. 

But non pre-emptive means that if we took out a process from the ready queue and gave it to the CPU to get it executed, then that process will get completely executed. This means however much its burst time is—we talked about different times in the video before this, so you can check out that video—first, its entire burst time will get completed, and only after that will we bring some new process. 

If I explain this with a simple point of view: we placed a process in the running state from the ready queue. Running means we give it to the CPU. Now, this process went into which state? Let's say it was process P1; process P1 came into running now. Now, if I place a process again in the ready queue from the running queue—meaning I placed P1 again in the ready queue—what is the reason for placing it again in the ready queue? 

The first reason can be the time quantum, where we start to execute some new process while stopping the running one in between. The reason is the time quantum; the CPU has already decided to execute a process only for 2 seconds. So if a process arrived and it's saying it needs 5 seconds, I'll run it only for 2 seconds and then will place it in the ready queue. Then I'll bring a different process for 2 seconds, then a third one for 2 seconds, and so on. 

If I explain with a simple example: let's say there's a class scenario, and there are 10 students in that class. Every student has 5 questions to ask. As a teacher, if I'm there, I'm acting as a processor, and those 10 students are processes. Every student has 5 questions to ask. So I reach the first student, S1 asks me 5 questions, I answer all of them, and then I go to S2. Then I take 5 questions from S2, I solve them, and then reach student 3. If I'm moving this way, then it's non pre-emptive. This means I'll execute a process completely and then I'll move to another process. 

But in pre-emptive, it's possible that I reach S1, take its question 1, solve its Q1 and Q2, and when it is about to ask Q3, I pre-empt. This means the CPU got pre-empted there and moved to student 2. I take Q1 and Q2 of student 2, and when it is about to ask Q3, I get pre-empted to S3. This means it's the CPU that is getting pre-empted here, but we are pre-empting the process because of it. Pre-empting a process means we'll place that process again in the ready queue and will bring it back in the running queue in the future. 

So one reason for why we do this is the time quantum. This means I already decided that I'll do only 2 questions of every student at a time. When it gets its turn again after taking 2 questions from 10 students, then its turn will come again, and then I'll take questions 3 and 4 again. After taking questions 3 and 4 from everyone, I'll take question 5 again. One advantage of this here is responsiveness. This means if I'm taking all 5 questions of every student, then student 10's turn will come very late. But if I'm taking just 2 questions each, then student 10—i.e., the last student—will also get its turn faster. So we use it for responsiveness; that is our pre-emptive scheduling. 

The other reason why we do pre-emption could be priority. Priority means, like, if I started to attend to the questions of student 1, I took question 1, solved question 2, and until then a high-priority process came. A high-priority process means, let's say, the topper of that class came and is cutting in between and asking me to solve his question. Then maybe I'd give more priority to him. If I give priority to him, then I'll ask student 1 to wait as I'm taking another process's question. So many times, because of priority also, we bring back a running process again into the ready queue. 

And another reason could be maybe if we have to execute a process for less time, then we can categorize it. This means one student came and said he wants to ask a question, and another student said he wants to ask 10 questions. If the one who wants to ask 10 questions came first, or both came at the same time—if both come at the same time, maybe I get a little biased there and say, "First, those ask who have just one question, so I'll solve it fast and make him free, because the one with 10 questions needs more time." So maybe I'd pick the shortest one first. These are just different criteria that we use actually to get the processes executed. 

So here, pre-emptive is set. Non pre-emptive means: if we took out a process from the ready queue and placed it in the running queue, if that process came in the running queue, then it'll get executed completely. It won't go back in the ready queue; it'll get terminated directly after it. So that is what non pre-emptive is. 

In pre-emptive scheduling, we have algorithms like SRTF (Shortest Remaining Time First / Shortest Job First), LRTF (Longest Remaining Time First), and Round Robin. Other than this, non pre-emptive includes FCFS (First Come First Serve), Shortest Job First, Longest Job First, and Highest Response Ratio Next.
