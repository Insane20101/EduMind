First, I'll designate a `header` tag for the top section of my website. Inside this `header`, I'll create a navigation bar using a `nav` tag. Within the `nav`, I'll make an unordered list (`ul`) with list items (`li`) for navigation links, such as 'Home,' 'About,' and 'Contact.'

```html
<header>

            <li>About</li>
            <li>Contact</li>
        </ul>
    </nav>
</header>
```

Anyone visiting my website will clearly see this as the header and navigation bar because I've used the `header` and `nav` tags. Now, let's say I want a footer. My main content will come after the header, so I'll use a `main` tag for that. After the `main` content, I'll add a `footer` tag.

Let's review some specific semantic tags:
*   **`header`**: Used to represent the top section of a web page.
*   **`nav`**: Contains navigation links.
*   **`article`**: Indicates a self-contained piece of content, such as a blog post or news article. If you have a particular, self-contained section, put it in an `article` tag.
*   **`section`**: Represents a thematic grouping of content. If one section on your page discusses one topic, and another section discusses something else, you'd use separate `section` tags.
*   **`aside`**: Typically used for sidebars or content tangentially related to the main content. For example, you might put 'About the Author' notes in an `aside` tag, as seen in many blogs.
*   **`footer`**: As discussed, used for copyright information, important links, and other content at the bottom of the page.
*   **`figure` and `figcaption`**: Used when you want to include images, diagrams, or charts. `figure` wraps the content, and `figcaption` provides a caption.
*   **`main`**: Represents the dominant content area of the `body` of a document.
*   **`time`**: Used to represent time-related information, such as dates or specific times.

These tags are used to enhance SEO and improve accessibility. They also allow browsers to make informed decisions about our page. For example, if a browser has a feature to read content aloud (like Microsoft Edge), it can decide to only read the main content. You wouldn't want it to read 'code with Harry, home, courses, tutorial' aloud; you'd want it to read the primary content: 'HTML, Semantic Tags.'

Semantic tags were introduced in HTML5. The primary reason for their introduction was to arrange things more effectively, making it easier for browsers, crawlers, Google, Bing, and other search engines, or anyone parsing the content or generating a preview (like when you share a URL on Twitter), to understand your content properly. If you want your content to be understood properly, you will use semantic tags.

This image beautifully illustrates the concept: HTML without semantic tags often looks like a collection of generic `div`s, whereas HTML with semantic tags clearly outlines the `header`, `nav`, and `aside`. It's important not to confuse semantic tags with styling. They are not used for visual design or positioning elements. You use CSS to style things and arrange them visually. Semantic tags are purely for defining the meaning and structure of your content, indicating which part is the header, footer, or main content. Semantic tags do not perform any magic; they are solely made for accessibility and to help understand your page's structure.
