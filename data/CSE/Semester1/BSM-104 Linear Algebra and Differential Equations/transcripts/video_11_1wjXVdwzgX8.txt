### Characteristic Equation and Eigenvalues

The matrix formed has a determinant in the form of an equation, which is called the characteristic equation. Upon solving the characteristic equation, we get the value of lambda, which represents the eigenvalue.

Let's look at an example. When we multiply lambda inside, we get lambda in the places of 1, so we directly subtract lambda from the diagonal elements of the matrix. The determinant will be calculated. This is the characteristic polynomial, and when we equate it to zero, it becomes the characteristic equation. ### Linear Independence, Multiplicity, and Related Concepts

When three eigenvalues are distinct, for example 1, 2, and 3, their corresponding eigenvectors will be linearly independent. I'll explain this with an example in the next video. If two eigenvalues are the same, their corresponding eigenvectors might be linearly independent or linearly dependent. This concept falls under another topic called diagonalization, which we will discuss in the next class.

Eigenvalues are also known as characteristic values, eigen roots, characteristic roots, or latent roots. The set of eigenvalues is called the spectrum of matrix A, and the largest eigenvalue is called the spectral radius of A. These concepts lead us to another topic: diagonalization. In the next class, we'll discuss it with an example.

For example, if the eigenvalues of a matrix are 1, 2, and 3, these values are repeating only once, so the algebraic multiplicity of each of these values will be 1. Now, if we have eigenvalues 1, 1, 3, since 1 is repeating twice, its algebraic multiplicity will be 2, and the algebraic multiplicity of 3 will be 1. When we write this in equation form, we get the same form. So, algebraic multiplicity refers to the power of the root in the characteristic equation.

Geometric multiplicity is related to eigenvectors; it is the number of linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to a particular eigenvalue. The algebraic multiplicity is always greater than or equal to the geometric multiplicity.

### Properties of Eigenvalues: Determinant and Trace

For a 3x3 matrix with eigenvalues lambda 1, lambda 2, and lambda 3, the determinant will be equal to the product of its eigenvalues. The trace of the matrix is the sum of its diagonal elements. Therefore, the trace is equal to the sum of the eigenvalues. For example, if the trace is 7, it will be equal to the sum of the eigenvalues.
